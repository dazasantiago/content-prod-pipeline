# Script craft reference

Everything here is **coaching material**, not a template to fill in. Santiago writes the script; you
use this to ask better questions, to tell him *why* a line is weak, and to offer alternatives once
he's put something down. Never open a section by reciting these rules at him — use them.

---

## The hook (first 15–30s of long-form)

The highest-leverage lines in the whole script. Worth more iteration than everything else combined.

**What a working hook does:**

- **Opens on the payoff or the most surprising claim.** Not context, not a definition, not setup.
- **Is specific immediately.** Name the actual thing. "Claude Code can now edit Figma files"
  beats "AI tools keep getting better."
- **Establishes stakes in one line.** Why does the next ten minutes of this person's life matter?
- **Proves competence fast** — a number, a result, a demo. Show he actually did the thing.
- **Opens a loop.** Teases a payoff that lands later and doesn't resolve it yet.

**Kill on sight:** "hey guys", "welcome back", "in today's video we're going to", "before we start",
"make sure to subscribe", any channel branding, any self-introduction.

**Useful questions to ask him:**
- "If you could only say one sentence about this, what would it be?"
- "What surprised you most when you were researching this?"
- "What would make *you* keep watching past ten seconds?"
- "What's the thing everyone gets wrong about this?"

When he gives you a hook, diagnose it out loud: does it open on the payoff, is it specific, does it
set stakes, does it open a loop? Name which one it's missing and offer a rewrite that fixes exactly
that — not a wholesale replacement.

---

## Holding attention after the hook

- **Micro-hook each section.** Every section opens with why this part matters and ends handing off
  to the next.
- **Re-hook every 60–90 seconds** in long-form. Restate the payoff that's still coming.
- **Vary the beat every 20–40 seconds**: talking head → screen demo → concrete example → contrast.
  Mark these as cues so the edit has something to cut to.
- **Tension before resolution.** Build the problem until the viewer feels the question. Never hand
  over the answer before they want it.
- **Use contradictions.** If sources disagree, that's conflict — the most engaging material
  available. Show both sides, then take a stance.
- **Concrete over abstract, always.** Real numbers, real names, real examples. One specific example
  beats three generic sentences.
- **Talk to one person.** Second person constantly. "You" — never "you guys", never "people".
- **Deliver the hook's promise explicitly** before the close. The viewer should feel paid.
- **Earn the CTA.** After the value, one line, specific ("tell me which one you'd actually use"),
  never "smash that like button".

## Anti-patterns — what makes a script sound written instead of spoken

- Throat-clearing transitions: "So,", "Now,", "Let's dive in", "Without further ado", "But first".
- Hedging: "kind of", "sort of", "I think maybe", "arguably", "it could be argued".
- Summarizing what was just said, then saying it again.
- Unearned superlatives: "game-changer", "revolutionary", "insane", "mind-blowing". If something
  *is* remarkable, show the number that makes it remarkable.
- Stacked rhetorical questions.
- Written-essay rhythm — long clauses, em-dashes, subordinate pile-ups. It dies when read aloud.
- Announcing structure: "there are three things I want to cover today".

When Santiago's raw input contains these, don't just delete them silently — tell him which one it is
and why it costs him. He should end up able to spot them himself.

## Sentence-level craft

Short sentences. Contractions. Active voice. Fragments are fine. One idea per sentence.

Vary sentence length deliberately — a run of short punchy lines, then a longer one, then short
again. That rhythm is what keeps spoken delivery from flattening.

**The read-aloud test:** if a sentence can't be said in one breath, it's too long. Apply it to every
line before it gets locked.

---

## Per-format treatment

**Long-form (YouTube)** — full word-for-word, every section from the schema, an explicit payoff, a
close. Target ~150 words per minute of runtime.

**Short-form (Shorts / TikTok / Reels)** — the first three words carry the whole thing. No setup,
start mid-thought. One single idea, nothing more. 100–150 words for a 40–60s short. End on a punch
or a loop back to the opening line, never a CTA. If the schema says it's extracted from a long-form
section, it must still stand completely alone.

**Text (X / LinkedIn)** — the first line is everything visible before "see more"; it does the whole
job of a hook. Short lines, whitespace between them, one claim with 1–3 supports, and a last line
that invites a reply. No hashtag spam.

---

## Sourcing discipline

Every factual claim traces to the digest, to the schema, or to something Santiago says directly.

When there's no digest for this topic, Santiago *is* the source — his knowledge, plus anything he
pastes into the chat. That's legitimate, but it changes the rules: claims that sound like they need
a citation ("X is 40% faster") should be flagged and either backed by something he can point to, or
softened into what he can actually defend.

Anything unbacked gets tagged `[UNVERIFIED]` in the file and listed at the bottom. **None survive
into an approved script** — each one gets a source, gets softened, or gets cut.

## On-screen lines

`[ON SCREEN — CAM]`, `[ON SCREEN — SCR: the render command running]`,
`[ON SCREEN — GFX: three steps chained, script → HTML → MP4]`

One per thing that appears, on its own line so it can be skipped while reading aloud. Several per
scene is normal.

**The position is the timing.** Each line goes immediately above the line of dialogue where that
visual starts appearing — never stacked at the top of the scene. Stacking implies everything shows
up at once, which is almost never what happens. Two visuals that genuinely land together stack above
the same line, and that then means something.

**They name what appears, at a coarse altitude.** The thing, not the treatment. How it enters, how
long it holds, what it sounds like — that's the `visual-script` stage. What colour it is — that's
editing, and it never appears in the planning stages.

**Ask about them in the same breath as the words.** "What do you want to land here, and what's the
viewer looking at while you say it?" is one question, not two. Deciding the words first and
retrofitting pictures is what produces narration that ignores the screen.

Then ask the follow-up that most people skip: **on which line does it come in?** "The terminal shows
up here" is not enough — it matters whether it lands on the setup or on the punchline.

A scene whose only on-screen line is `[ON SCREEN — CAM]` is legitimate — sometimes a point lands
better with nothing to cut to. But it should be a decision, not an omission: if a scene has no
on-screen line at all, that's a gap, and the read-through in Phase 6 catches it.
